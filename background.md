## Context

A talking bear can wear clothes, go to school, and have an entire personality invented for it. Someone also decides whether to call that bear “he” or “she.” That choice is easy to overlook, which is what drew us to animal characters in the first place. If you pictured the bear as a boy, would you have made the same assumption about a cat?

## Research questions

Our project asks a variety of questions!

How did gender representation among animal characters in popular English-language picture books change from the 1950s through the 1990s? We also wanted to see what the overall totals might hide. Did female characters become more common with each decade? Were some animals consistently more likely to be portrayed as male?

To explore these questions, we use two datasets from The Pudding's [_Bears Will Be Boys_](https://pudding.cool/2025/07/kids-books/). The first records how picture books refer to animal characters, including pronouns such as “she” and “he” and family roles such as “mother” or “brother.” Our analysis focuses on books published from 1950 through 1999. The second dataset comes from a survey in which respondents finished a short story about one of seven animals. Comparing the two lets us ask whether the same animals tend to be called “he” in published books and in stories people make up themselves. Whether particular books influenced those choices remains an open question.

## Why it matters

Picture books give children early examples of who gets to lead an adventure, solve a problem, or look after someone else. For us, counting characters is a starting point. A book might include three female animals and still give the only male character every decision that moves the story forward. A count would tell us who appears. To understand who gets to act, we would need to read the story.

Animal characters raise another question. If a book calls a character “the bear” from beginning to end, readers can still picture that bear as male or female. That gap between what appears on the page and what readers bring to it is why we included the storytelling survey.

## What's already known

Research on children’s literature has repeatedly found that male characters receive more representation than female characters. McCabe and colleagues’ 2011 study examined 5,618 children’s books published in the United States between 1900 and 2000 (McCabe et al. 197). Male characters appeared more often in titles and central roles, with the greatest imbalance among animal characters (McCabe et al. 211). The study also found that change was uneven across the century (McCabe et al. 197). This matters for our project because we can't simply assume that each new decade brought more equal representation.

Additionally, Helen Adam and Laurie J. Harper focused on frequently read picture books in eight early learning centres in Australia and the United States. Most of the books shared by educators in their study presented traditional, binary, and stereotypical views of gender (Adam and Harper 453). Their research brings attention to the books children actually encounter in educational settings, as well as the roles those books make available to different characters.

Jennifer Lindsay’s 2023 study, however, adds another complication. A character presented as neutral may not be interpreted that way. In a study involving 122 undergraduate students, participants wrote stories about images of picture-book characters. They more often assumed that neutral characters were male (Lindsay 119). For our project, this distinction is especially useful. What a book explicitly tells us about a character’s gender and what a reader assumes aren't necessarily the same thing. Our analysis builds on these studies by analyzing differences across animal species and publication decades, while also using the survey responses to consider how people assign gender in their own stories.

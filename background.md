# Background

## Research question

Our project asks a variety of questions, a few being how the gender representation of animal characters in popular English-language picture books changed between the 1950s and 1990s, and whether certain animals were more likely to be portrayed as male or female. We are interested in what becomes visible when we look beyond the overall totals. Did female representation increase consistently across decades, did some animals remain mostly male, etc.?

To explore these questions, we use the book and storytelling survey datasets from The Pudding’s _Bears Will Be Boys_. Our book analysis focuses on titles published from 1950 through 1999. We also compare those portrayals with the pronouns survey respondents chose when completing short stories about animals. This lets us study gender patterns in published stories alongside the choices people make when imagining characters themselves. The comparison can't establish that reading particular books caused those choices, but it gives us another way to investigate when “he” becomes the default.

## Why it matters

A talking bear can wear clothes, go to school, and have an entire personality invented for it. Its gender is also a choice, even when that choice is easy to overlook. That is what drew us to animal characters. Making a character an animal doesn't automatically remove human assumptions about gender.

Picture books are one of the very first places where children encounter ideas about who gets to lead, have adventures, or care for others. When male characters appear more often, children have fewer opportunities to see female characters occupying the same range of roles. Earlier research identifies this imbalance as a concern for how children come to understand gender and their own possibilities.

For us, counting characters is a starting point. A book could include several female animals while giving them little to do, and a character without a stated gender might still be imagined as male. We want to understand both the patterns our data reveals and what the numbers leave unanswered. Looking across decades and animal species helps us ask more specific questions about whose stories are being told and which assumptions seem ordinary enough to go unnoticed.

## What's already known

Research on children’s literature has repeatedly found that male characters receive more representation than female characters. McCabe and colleagues’ 2011 study examined 5,618 children’s books published in the United States between 1900 and 2000. Male characters appeared more often in titles and central roles, with the greatest imbalance among animal characters. The study also found that change was uneven across the century. This matters for our project because we can't simply assume that each new decade brought more equal representation.

Additionally, Helen Adam and Laurie J. Harper focused on frequently read picture books in 8 early learning centres in Australia and the United States. Most of the books shared by educators in their study presented traditional, binary, and stereotypical views of gender. Their research brings attention to the books children actually encounter in educational settings, as well as the roles those books make available to different characters.

Jennifer Lindsay’s 2023 study, however, adds another complication. A character presented as neutral may not be interpreted that way. In a study involving 122 undergraduate students, participants wrote stories about images of picture-book characters. They more often assumed that neutral characters were male. For our project, this distinction is especially useful. What a book explicitly tells us about a character’s gender and what a reader assumes aren't necessarily the same thing. Our analysis builds on these studies by analyzing differences across animal species and publication decades, while also using the survey responses to consider how people assign gender in their own stories.

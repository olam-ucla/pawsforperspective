# Methods

## Data sources

The datasets we are working with are kids-book-animals.csv and survey-animal-stories.csv from the “Bears Will Be Boys” dataset on the Pudding GitHub page. The kids-book-animals data includes the book title, animal, animal group, pronoun, author, publication year and decade, publisher, ISBN, Goodreads link, description, cover image, rating count, average rating, and review count. This dataset gives us a comprehensive overview of the books. The dataset can illuminate which animals skew male or female in popular picture books (bears, rabbits, and mice skew male; birds, ducks, and cats skew female), whether the male skew shifts across decades, how gender patterns relate to popularity, authorship, and publishers, and the overall ratio: about 66% he/him, 31% she/her, and 2.6% other. The survey-animal-stories.csv includes information from people who participated in the Pudding survey. It has information on the animal shown in the prompt and how participants responded when gendering the creature. This dataset could tell us about people’s natural tendencies regarding gender in animals. What these datasets cannot tell us is why authors chose these genders, how children or parents read the characters, gender in illustrations or in unpronounced animals, non-binary or ambiguous representation beyond a thin “it” category, whether less-rated books look different and anything about causes, since the dataset only describes patterns. 
The data was generated by selecting any book that had “animals” as one of its top Goodreads tags, tagged as “animals” on the Children’s Books Database, and any book that GPT-4o identified as featuring animals. Every book chosen was then manually evaluated to see each book’s representation of animals. It's important to note that they excluded anthologies and collections, such as Grimm’s Fairy Tales.
The original sources of our dataset, Bears Will Be Boys, were popular English-language children’s picture books. The researchers who compiled this data used Goodreads (a popular social networking and cataloging platform) ratings, tags, and reading lists, along with the New York Times children's picture book bestseller list and Miami University’s Children’s Book Database, to select books. Additionally, they chose around 30 books per decade starting in 1950 (they also added in some older classics)! The researchers then read the books and recorded the gender of various animal characters based on factors like their pronouns and other gender-specific language. They also made sure to conduct a separate survey to figure out how people assign gender to different animals.
The creation of the dataset was funded by The Pudding, a digital publication website allowing teams to visualize cultural debates, social issues, and pop culture trends. The Pudding avoids publishing complex topics in the form of long essays. Instead, their goal is to visualize those ideas to inform and entertain diverse audiences. This is an independent operation, founded in 2017, and connected to the creative data design studio Polygraph, an in-house brand that powers and funds The Pudding. The Pudding is a purely editorial, ad-free publication that focuses on deep-dive visual essays and data journalism. The main Pudding team also collaborates with freelance journalists, data journalists, and experts on mainstream cultural ideas. To fund this website and its staff, the Polygraph team operates as a commercial B2B studio. 
In the dataset, we do not know the animals’ personalities, appearance, size, or cultural associations that could influence the perception of gender. In addition, we do not know what kind of roles female and male animals portray in the children's books. For example, if certain genders claimed more leadership, caregiving roles, or brave, intelligent characteristics. An important point that is missing that could heavily influence what animals are gendered is the author's gender, cultural background, intentions, or experiences. The spreadsheet is quite limited since the dataset only includes popular children’s books listed on Goodreads. The dataset does not include less popular, underrepresented books. Without these details, there is the possibility of biases and overgeneralized findings.

# Methods

Our project, *Paws for Perspective*, explores the historical evolution and cultural execution of gender representation among animal characters in popular English-language children's picture books [1-3]. While most interpretations of literature often focus on human protagonists, nonhuman characters carry deep-seated social weight in early childhood socialization, commonly serving as unspoken conduits for gender norms and societal expectations [2, 4, 5].

To analyze these patterns, our digital methodology addresses **the three levels of a Digital Humanities project**: **Sources**, **Processing**, and **Presentation**. 

#### **1. Sources (Selection & Data Circulation)**
Our primary research relies on the *Bears Will Be Boys* dataset created by researchers at *The Pudding* and *Polygraph* [2, 7]. The sample contains **841 animal character records** across approximately **284 popular picture books** spanning from pre-1950 classics through the 2020s, accompanied by a **1,300 participant reader survey** [2, 3, 8]. To break this down, roughly **30 books per decade** were sampled through careful selection using Goodreads community tags and review counts, the *New York Times* Children's Picture Book Bestseller List, and Miami University's Children's Book Database [2, 9].

To preserve analytical consistency across decades of publication, strict methodological boundaries and exclusions were established [2, 10]:
* **Excluded Animal Categories**: Story collections and anthologies (such as *Grimm's Fairy Tales*) were excluded to focus on single picture book narratives [9, 10].
* **Excluded Animal Categories**: Animals referenced exclusively in plural groups (e.g., "the birds") and species names that indicate biological sex (e.g., cow, hen, rooster, gander) were removed [10].
* **Excluded Visual & Implicit Cues**: Character gender was strictly coded from directly articulated pronouns in the text, such as he/him, she/her, and it/they [2, 10]. Non-linguistic indicators, for example, illustrations, character clothing, and names, were intentionally left out [10]. As shown by scholars focusing on visual culture, visual cues can project a "masculine default" even when text remains neutral [11-13].
* **Excluded Metadata**: Human characters, author/illustrator personal demographics, and library circulation numbers were excluded from the primary dataset [10].
